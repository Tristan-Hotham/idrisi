Al-Idrisi's World

In 1154 a Muslim scholar at the Norman court of Sicily finished a map of the known world for King Roger II. It has south at the top, seven climates running across it like shelves, and some {{source.n|int}} towns I could read and place. I set it beside a modern map to see where it is right, where it bends, and how it compares with the Christian Hereford map drawn about {{source.years_apart|int}} years later.

The map is upside down, at least to us. Al-Idrisi, a scholar born in Ceuta, put south at the top, as many mapmakers in the Islamic world did, so Arabia and Africa sit above the Mediterranean and Europe hangs underneath. He drew it in Palermo for Roger II, the Norman king of Sicily, and wrote a long book to go with it, the Book of Roger, which walks across the known world in seventy sections: seven climates, bands of latitude from the equator northwards, each cut into ten pieces from west to east. Laid side by side, the sections make one great rectangle of the world, from the Atlantic to China.

The original silver map is lost. In 1928 the German scholar Konrad Miller rebuilt it from the manuscripts as one sheet, with every name transcribed. I read {{source.n|int}} of its towns on Miller's reconstruction, {{source.high|int}} clearly and {{source.medium|int}} with some doubt, found where each one really is, and measured how the map bends the world. Click a town on either map to see it on the other, or turn the old map north up.

    02 · Seven climatesA world on shelves

    Al-Idrisi's world is a grid. The red lines that run straight across the map divide it into the seven climates of Greek and Islamic geography, bands of latitude from the equator in the south to the cold north, and each climate is drawn the same height, about {{climate_summary.height_px|int}} pixels on Miller's sheet. The climates work: the median latitude of the towns rises from climate to climate without a break, from {{climates.1.lat_median|f1}}° in the first, with Aden, Sanaa and Aswan, to {{climates.6.lat_median|f1}}° in the sixth, with Paris, London and Constantinople, and {{climates.7.lat_median|f1}}° in the seventh, where only three English towns are legible.

    But the shelves are not the same depth in reality. The latitudes that best separate the towns of one climate from the next fall at about {{climate_summary.bounds.0|f1}}°, {{climate_summary.bounds.1|f1}}°, {{climate_summary.bounds.2|f1}}°, {{climate_summary.bounds.3|f1}}°, {{climate_summary.bounds.4|f1}}° and {{climate_summary.bounds.5|f1}}° north. The second climate covers {{climate_summary.south_width|f1}} degrees of latitude; the middle and northern ones an average of {{climate_summary.north_mid_width|f1}}. So the south is squeezed and the Mediterranean and Europe, where the towns are thick, are spread out. By those boundaries {{climate_summary.right_climate_pct|int}}% of towns sit in the right climate and every one is within one climate of it.

    Each dot is a located town, placed at its true latitude (across) in the climate the map puts it in (up). Dashed lines: the latitudes that best separate neighbouring climates. Hover a dot.

    Within each climate the map behaves like a rectangular grid of degrees. Fitted as a whole, it draws a degree of longitude {{affine.lon_lat_ratio|f2}} times as long as a degree of latitude, as if the whole world lay at about {{affine.true_scale_lat|int}}° north, and the fit puts the median town {{affine.median_resid_km|int}} km from where it is. Longitude is easier to get right than latitude here: the fit explains {{affine.r2_lon|f2}} of the spread in longitude and {{affine.r2_lat|f2}} in latitude, because the climates squeeze and stretch the north to south direction.

    03 · Direction and distanceRoughly right everywhere, best at home

    Turned north up, the map points the right way. Across all {{pairs.n|int}} pairs of located towns at least 100 km apart, the median error in direction is {{pairs.median_brg_err|int}} degrees and {{pairs.within45_pct|int}}% are within 45 degrees. Distances keep their order too: the rank correlation between distance on the map and on the ground is {{pairs.spearman_dist|f2}}.

    Where it goes wrong is a matter of neighbourhoods. For each town I took its eight nearest real neighbours, fitted the best simple stretch and turn from the map to the ground on them alone, and asked where they put the town. The median miss is {{local.median_km|int}} km, and {{local.within100_pct|int}}% are within 100 km. But it varies a great deal. Sicily is the most faithful region on the map, with a median local error of {{regions.Sicily and Malta.median_local_km|int}} km, followed by {{rank_local.1|raw}} ({{regions.Syria and Palestine.median_local_km|int}} km), {{rank_local.2|raw}} and {{rank_local.3|raw}}. The edges are worst: Arabia ({{regions.Arabia.median_local_km|int}} km), central and eastern Europe ({{regions.Central and Eastern Europe.median_local_km|int}} km) and Sind and India ({{regions.Sind and India.median_local_km|int}} km).

    The Mediterranean world is drawn about twice as faithfully as Asia beyond the Euphrates: a median local error of {{groups.Mediterranean.median_local_km|int}} km against {{groups.Asia beyond the Euphrates.median_local_km|int}} km.

    Northern Europe sits in between, at {{groups.Northern Europe.median_local_km|int}} km. That fits the way al-Idrisi says, in his preface, that the work was done: by gathering the reports of travellers who came to Roger's court. The coasts that ships from Palermo sailed were described by many of them; the far east and the far north by few.

    04 · SicilyThe king's island, drawn biggest

    Sicily is not only the best drawn region. It is also the biggest. Measured the same way as in my study of the Hereford map, comparing distances between pairs of towns on the map and on the ground against the map's average, Sicily is drawn at {{regions.Sicily and Malta.stretch|f2}} times the average scale, more than any other region: Greece and the Balkans {{regions.Greece and the Balkans.stretch|f2}}, Italy and England {{regions.Italy and Corsica.stretch|f2}}, Syria and Palestine {{regions.Syria and Palestine.stretch|f2}}, and at the other end Morocco and Algeria {{regions.Morocco and Algeria.stretch|f2}} and Arabia {{regions.Arabia.stretch|f2}}. The rectangular grid alone would leave Sicily at {{regions.Sicily and Malta.grid_stretch|f2}}, so the enlargement is the mapmaker's choice. I read {{sicily.n|int}} towns on it, from Palermo, Messina and Syracuse to Butera and Caronia, more than on all of England and central Europe together.

    Its one fault is a turn. The best local fit rotates Sicily by about {{regions.Sicily and Malta.rotation|abs}} degrees anticlockwise, so its pairs of towns are off in direction by a median {{regions.Sicily and Malta.median_brg_err|int}} degrees, one of the larger figures on the map, while their spacing is the most accurate. Al-Idrisi had the island's shape right and set it slightly askew.

    05 · The NileFrom the Mountains of the Moon

    At the top of the map, beyond the equator, are the Mountains of the Moon, and below them the lakes from which the Nile flows north across the whole height of the map to the delta. The text is precise. The source of the Nile, it says, is in the Mountains of the Moon, "whose beginning is sixteen degrees beyond the equinoctial line", from which ten springs feed two lakes, whose rivers join in a third, very great lake. It also quotes Qudama that the Nile runs {{nile.text_length_miles|int}} miles from source to sea.

    The map does not show sixteen degrees. Miller's reconstruction puts the mountains only about {{nile.moon_px_south_of_equator|int}} pixels beyond the equator line, a sixth of the height of a climate. And {{nile.text_length_miles|int}} miles, at about 2 km to the Arab mile, is some {{routes.nile_km_at_lo|int}} to {{routes.nile_km_at_hi|int}} km, against about {{nile.nile_km_real|int}} km for the real river. Where the Nile runs through towns, though, the map is good. Fitted on the whole map, Aswan comes out at {{nile.aswan_fit_lat|f1}}° (really {{nile.aswan_lat|f1}}°), Alexandria at {{nile.alexandria_fit_lat|f1}}° (really {{nile.alexandria_lat|f1}}°), and Egypt spans {{nile.egypt_lat_span_fit|f1}} degrees of latitude on the map against {{nile.egypt_lat_span_real|f1}} in reality.

    06 · BritainAn island at the edge of the seventh climate

    England, "the island of Inkiltara", sits in the far north west of the map, astride the boundary between the sixth and seventh climates: London, Dover and Hastings in the sixth, Lincoln, Grimsby and Salisbury in the seventh. I could read {{britain.n|int}} English towns, too few for firm figures, but they tell a clear story. North to south, England is in the right place: on the map as a whole its towns come out, at the median, within a tenth of a degree of their true latitude. East to west it is pushed {{britain.lon_shift|f1}} degrees too far east, and the island is turned: the best local fit rotates it about {{regions.England.rotation|int}} degrees clockwise, so that Paris, really {{britain.london_paris_brg_true|int}} degrees from London, is drawn at {{britain.london_paris_brg_map|int}} degrees, nearly due south.

    Measured from Jerusalem, as in the Hereford study, the English towns point the right way to within a median {{jerusalem.england_brg_err|int}} degrees on al-Idrisi's map, against {{hereford.britain_brg_err|int}} for the British towns on the Hereford map, which was made in England. Hereford gives Britain {{hereford.britain_names_pct|f1}}% of its names and draws it at {{hereford.britain_stretch|f1}} times its average scale; al-Idrisi draws England at {{regions.England.stretch|f2}} times, more than France ({{regions.France and the Low Countries.stretch|f2}}) but less than Sicily.

    07 · Distances in the bookMiles, days and the road

    The Book of Roger is full of distances: so many miles from one town to the next, so many days' march. Dozy and de Goeje's 1866 edition of its chapters on North Africa and Spain gives the text in Arabic and French. I took {{routes.n|int}} distances between towns I could identify, {{routes.n_miles|int}} in miles and {{routes.n_days|int}} in day stages, and checked each against the scan line by line.

    They are consistent. The stated miles and the straight line distances rise together (rank correlation {{routes.spearman_miles|f2}}), at a median of {{routes.median_km_per_mile|f2}} km of straight line per stated mile, with half the routes between {{routes.q1_km_per_mile|f2}} and {{routes.q3_km_per_mile|f2}}. The Arab mile is usually put at about 2 km, so either the roads ran between a third and two fifths longer than the straight line, or the mile in these itineraries was shorter. A day stage is a median {{routes.median_km_per_day|int}} km in a straight line, and in all {{routes.n_both|int}} routes that give both, a day is about {{routes.median_miles_per_day|int}} miles.

    The map seems to follow the book. For the {{routes.n_on_map|int}} routes in miles whose two ends I located on the map, distance on the map tracks the stated miles (rank correlation {{routes.spearman_map_miles|f2}}) more closely than it tracks the real distance ({{routes.spearman_map_km|f2}}). It is a small sample, but it is what you would expect of a map put together from itineraries.

    08 · Two worldsPalermo and Hereford

    About {{source.years_apart|int}} years after al-Idrisi, a clergyman in Hereford drew the largest medieval map of the world that survives, with east at the top and Jerusalem at its centre. I measured it the same way in an earlier study. Measured from Jerusalem, with the best rotation and scaling, al-Idrisi's map explains {{jerusalem.sim_r2|f2}} of the spread of real positions, against {{hereford.fit_r2|f2}} for Hereford; the median town lands {{jerusalem.sim_median_disp_km|int}} km from where it is, against {{hereford.median_disp_km|int}} km. Directions from Jerusalem are right to a median {{jerusalem.median_brg_err|int}} degrees ({{hereford.bearing_median_err|int}} on Hereford), with {{jerusalem.within45_pct|int}}% within 45 degrees ({{hereford.bearing_within45_pct|int}}%).

    The comparison is not quite like for like. The Hereford sample is smaller and full of classical names copied from Roman books; al-Idrisi's is larger and mostly towns of his own century, many of them on trade routes. But the difference in purpose shows. Hereford is a picture of history and Scripture that spends its space on the Holy Land, drawn {{hereford.holyland_stretch|f1}} times its average scale. Al-Idrisi's map is a geographer's: Syria and Palestine get {{regions.Syria and Palestine.stretch|f2}} times, Mecca sits close to the middle of the map, in the second climate, while Arabia is drawn at the smallest scale of any region ({{regions.Arabia.stretch|f2}}), and the extra space goes to the island where the map was made. Both maps spend most on home.

    09 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    The map. Konrad Miller's 1928 reconstruction, from Wikimedia Commons (public domain), {{source.img_w|int}} by {{source.img_h|int}} pixels. It is a modern redrawing from the manuscripts, not the original, and Miller added the transcriptions, so every position here is a position on Miller's sheet. I read {{source.n|int}} town labels by eye, {{source.high|int}} clearly and {{source.medium|int}} with some doubt, and recorded the pixel of each town's symbol. Miller drew towns as small brown discs; I found {{source.discs|int}} of them automatically and snapped {{source.snapped|int}} of my readings ({{source.snapped_pct|int}}%) to the nearest disc, a median {{source.snap_median_px|f1}} pixels away. The others I checked by eye.

    Real places. I gave each town a modern position and checked it against three gazetteers: al-Thurayya, which maps the towns and roads of the early Islamic world, Pleiades for ancient places, and Natural Earth for modern towns. {{source.checked|int}} of the {{source.n|int}} ({{source.checked_pct|int}}%) match a gazetteer entry of the same name, a median {{source.check_median_km|f1}} km from my position; the rest are small towns the gazetteers do not list. I left out names I could not identify with confidence.

    Measures. All measurements are made with the map turned north up. The global fit is the best affine map from map position to longitude and latitude. Local error, direction between pairs and scale by region are defined in the paper; each region needs five located towns to be scored. The climate boundaries are the latitudes that best separate the towns of neighbouring climates. The Hereford comparison repeats exactly the similarity fit and Jerusalem bearings of that study. The distances come from Dozy and de Goeje (1866), read in the Internet Archive's text and checked against the line they come from.

    What this is not. A sample of legible towns, not the whole map: rivers, mountains and peoples are left out, and coverage is thin where labels are few or unreadable, in India and central Asia, eastern Europe and Africa south of the Sahara ({{regions.Sind and India.n|int}}, {{regions.Central and Eastern Europe.n|int}} and {{regions.West Africa.n|int}} towns). The reconstruction is Miller's: where his redrawing differs from the manuscripts, so does my measurement. With clear readings only ({{high_only.n|int}} towns) the median local error is {{high_only.median_local_km|int}} km and the median global miss {{high_only.median_resid_km|int}} km, close to the figures for all towns.
